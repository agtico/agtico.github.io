BE — BLOOM ENERGY CORP

Consensus bullishness: -0.02

What The Business Is

Bloom Energy Corp. is an industrial power-equipment company focused on distributed, on-site electricity generation. Its core product is the Bloom Energy Server, a solid oxide fuel cell platform that converts fuel to electricity through an electrochemical, non-combustion process. ([bloomenergy.com](https://www.bloomenergy.com/resource/bloom-energy-server/?utm_source=openai)) The company sells power systems and attaches long-term service contracts; on the April 28, 2026 Q1 call, management said service attach rate is “100%,” with data-center service contracts typically averaging 10–15 years.

What It Does

Bloom’s systems are marketed as modular, fast-to-deploy, grid-independent power for customers such as data centers, semiconductor facilities, commercial/industrial sites, and AI infrastructure projects. Management’s current positioning is that Bloom can provide “time to power” faster than conventional grid interconnection, turbines, or diesel-backed solutions. In Q1 2026 commentary, CEO K.R. Sridhar said the company’s existing manufacturing footprint can support up to 5 GW of annual product capacity and that Bloom is adding capacity continuously rather than in one-off expansions. The Energy Server can run on natural gas or other fuels, and Bloom also markets hydrogen-capable fuel-cell and electrolyzer-related applications. ([investor.bloomenergy.com](https://investor.bloomenergy.com/press-releases/press-release-details/2024/Bloom-Energy-Announces-Hydrogen-Solid-Oxide-Fuel-Cell-with-60-Electrical-Efficiency-and-90-High-Temperature-Combined-Heat-and-Power-Efficiency/default.aspx?utm_source=openai))

Recent Highlights (Positive)

Bloom reported a record Q1 2026: revenue of $751.1 million versus $326.0 million a year earlier, non-GAAP operating income of $129.7 million, adjusted EBITDA of $143.0 million, non-GAAP diluted EPS of $0.44, and positive operating cash flow of $73.6 million. It raised 2026 revenue guidance to $3.4–$3.8 billion, with midpoint growth around 80%, and guided to roughly 34% non-GAAP gross margin. ([bloomenergy.com](https://www.bloomenergy.com/news/bloom-energy-reports-record-first-quarter-2026-results-and-raises-full-year-2026-guidance/?utm_source=openai))

The most material commercial development was the Oracle expansion: Bloom announced on April 13, 2026 that it would support up to 2.8 GW of Oracle AI/cloud infrastructure, and Oracle/BorderPlex later said Project Jupiter in New Mexico would use up to 2.45 GW of Bloom fuel-cell capacity. ([bloomenergy.com](https://www.bloomenergy.com/news/bloom-energy-and-oracle-expand-strategic-partnership-to-deploy-up-to-2-8-gw-to-accelerate-ai-infrastructure-build-out/?utm_source=openai)) On the call, management described Project Jupiter as “100% Bloom,” replacing previously planned gas turbines and backup diesel generators, and said more than half of current data-center backlog comes from non-Oracle hyperscalers, neo-clouds, and colocation customers.

On June 30, 2026, Bloom and Brookfield expanded their AI-infrastructure power financing partnership to $25 billion, a fivefold increase intended to accelerate global deployment of fuel-cell projects for AI power needs. ([investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/bloom-energy-brookfield-expand-ai-infrastructure-power-partnership-to-25-billion-4768832?utm_source=openai)) This helps address one key bottleneck for distributed power deployments: customer/project financing.

Recent Highlights (Negative)

The growth story is increasingly tied to AI data-center buildout and customer site readiness. In the Q1 call, management said Bloom is “not order constrained and not capacity constrained,” and that revenue pace depends on how fast customers can build greenfield sites. That shifts risk toward data-center permitting, construction schedules, AI demand durability, and whether large customers ultimately consume reserved capacity.

Bloom’s Q1 2026 10-Q flags multiple forward-looking sensitivities, including success in the AI data-center market, the rate of AI adoption, demand for data centers, production-capacity increases, cost reductions, financing availability, supply-chain/geopolitical issues, tariffs, government incentives, and changes tied to the Inflation Reduction Act and the “One Big Beautiful Bill Act.” ([companiesmarketcap.com](https://www.companiesmarketcap.com/eur/bloom-energy/sec-reports-10q/0001628280-26-028021/?utm_source=openai)) These risks matter because the company’s raised guidance assumes rapid scaling, manufacturing execution, and sustained demand.

Competition for AI power is intensifying. Recent market coverage notes that data-center operators are evaluating nuclear, renewables, fuel cells, and other on-site power options; separate commentary has pointed to competitive pressure from gas-generation, utility, nuclear, and large infrastructure players. ([investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/bloom-energy-brookfield-expand-ai-infrastructure-power-partnership-to-25-billion-4768832?utm_source=openai))

Finally, Bloom’s systems are cleaner than combustion-based local generation but often still rely on natural gas, so emissions, methane-supply concerns, local permitting, and customer decarbonization goals remain potential constraints. Industry coverage of AI power demand notes rising attention to carbon capture for gas-backed data-center power, underscoring that “clean power” claims remain scrutinized as AI electricity demand grows. ([axios.com](https://www.axios.com/2026/04/13/carbon-capture-ai-power-tech?utm_source=openai))
